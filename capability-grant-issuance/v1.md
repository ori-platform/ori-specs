# Capability Grant Issuance Contract v1

> Status: Design target, pre-ratification. No shipped consumer claims
> conformance. Amendable in place under the
> [pre-ratification exception](../VERSIONING.md#pre-ratification-exception).
>
> [capability-grant/v1.md](../capability-grant/v1.md) defines what a grant
> says and how a consumer verifies one. Nothing in that contract produces a
> legitimate grant, and nothing owns the key that signs one; a contract that
> specifies verification without issuance is half a contract. This document
> owns the other half: who issues, in what order, under what custody, how
> keys rotate, how a mistake is withdrawn, and what a package with no grant
> may do. Related: [runtime-release-bundle/v1.md](../runtime-release-bundle/v1.md)
> for the signing-ceremony and key-registry precedent this contract follows
> rather than reinventing, and [signing/v1.md](../signing/v1.md) for the
> author and Hub signatures the authority signature is distinct from.

## A package with no grant operates at an implicit Tier A ceiling

Decided here so it is a rule rather than a discovery: **a community package
that carries no `grant.json` is not unauthorised — it is unprivileged.** The
consumer's branch is explicit:

1. Archive admission and the manifest requirement run for every package —
   the path, duplicate, entry-kind and root-manifest rules of
   [capability-grant/v1.md](../capability-grant/v1.md) step 1, with the
   grant requirement relaxed to at most one.
2. **No root `grant.json`**: the package loads under an implicit ceiling
   exactly equivalent to a verified grant with `max_tier: A` whose permitted
   lists contain every trigger and action the manifest declares. Package
   admission still runs against the archive's manifest: a declaration at any
   tier above A refuses at load exactly as an under-granted package does. No
   grant verification and no denial processing occur, because there is no
   grant to verify or deny.
3. **Root `grant.json` present**: the full grant pipeline runs — stages 2–9
   of capability-grant/v1, with denial evaluated at its stated point.

The alternative — a grant required for everything — would make issuance a
review bottleneck on every publication, including packages that only send
alerts, while adding nothing: Tier A actions are informational and already
floored by the action registry. The durable migration rule: **existing
Tier A packages need no retrofit; every existing Tier B or C package
requires a grant before enforcement is enabled.** Issuance is the gate to
Tier B and C, which is the gate it is worth staffing.

## Issuance workflow

A grant is issued by the **capability authority** — a review function, not a
service. The Hub, the SDK, and the fleet manager are each forbidden from
signing grants; the Hub's artifact signature attests distribution, never
authority, and a provisioning backend's selection of a package by digest
confers nothing.

Issuance reviews one exact payload for one requested ceiling. The reviewer
examines the package against the criteria for the tier requested — what the
hooks do, what the actions actuate, what a malicious or defective version of
this exact payload could reach at that ceiling — and issues at the requested
ceiling, at a lower one, or not at all.

Every issuance appends an **audit record** retained with the authority's
evidence, outside any repository. Retention location does not relax the
schema: this is an evidence artifact, and it is closed, typed, and
fixture-validated like any other. One strict JSON object:

| Field | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `schema` | `string` | Exactly `ori.capability_grant_audit.v1`. |
| `reviewer` | `string` | The identity that performed the review, namespaced as `<scheme>:<identifier>` — lowercase scheme matching `^[a-z0-9][a-z0-9._-]*$`, one colon, non-empty identifier (e.g. `github:reviewer-login`). A bare name with no namespace is refused. |
| `skill_name`, `skill_version` | `string` | The identity granted, under the manifest identity rules. |
| `payload_sha256` | `string` | `sha256:<64-lowercase-hex>` — the exact payload tree digest reviewed, the same value the grant binds. |
| `requested_ceiling` | `object` | What the author asked for: `permitted_triggers`, `permitted_actions`, `max_tier`, each under the grant's own field rules. |
| `granted_ceiling` | `object` | What was issued, same shape. Every difference is a decision the record shows: a removed trigger, a removed action, a lowered tier. The granted ceiling MUST be contained in the requested one — trigger and action lists subsets, `max_tier` no higher — because issuance narrows, and a record showing a grant broader than its request is evidence of a defect, not a decision. |
| `grant_sha256` | `string` | SHA-256 over the issued grant's canonical unsigned bytes. |
| `issued_at_ms` | `integer` | Unix milliseconds, UTC, positive. |

A ceiling is all three components, not the tier alone: whether the reviewer
removed a trigger or an action permission must be reconstructible from the
record. **The record is bound to the grant it documents**: `skill_name`,
`skill_version`, `payload_sha256`, and the complete `granted_ceiling` MUST
equal the issued grant's own fields, and `grant_sha256` MUST equal the
SHA-256 of that grant's canonical unsigned bytes. A record that identifies
one grant while describing another authority ceiling is worse than no
record, because it answers "who authorised this" with a falsehood; a
validator refuses it, naming the field that disagrees.

Two properties of the record are evidentiary, not syntactic. The `reviewer`
string MUST originate from authenticated approval inside the protected
issuance environment — the ceremony's own short-lived credentials, bound to
the approval act — never from caller-supplied input: `github:alice` typed
into a JSON document is syntax, not evidence that Alice authorised
anything. And retention MUST be append-only or tamper-evident: an audit
trail that can be rewritten answers questions with whatever it was last
edited to say.

"Who authorised this skill to actuate" must have an answer months later,
and the answer is this record, not memory. The corpus carries validation
fixtures for the record — accepted ones bound to genuinely issued grants
from the capability-grant corpus, and refusals for the malformed, for a
granted ceiling exceeding its request, and for every binding field
disagreeing with the grant it names.

## Signing order

The steps are ordered so that each signature's input is fixed before the
signature exists, and no digest covers bytes produced after it. An
implementation that reorders them produces a grant that verifies locally and
fails in the runtime.

1. **The author finalises the payload**: every file the package will carry
   except `grant.json`.
2. **The author signs the manifest** — [signing/v1.md](../signing/v1.md)
   Profile 2, over the canonicalised `skill.yaml` alone.
3. **The authority computes the payload tree digest** over the archive's
   admitted entries, excluding exactly the root `grant.json`, per the digest
   domain in [capability-grant/v1.md](../capability-grant/v1.md). At this
   point no `grant.json` exists, so the exclusion is vacuous and the digest
   is over everything the author finalised.
4. **The authority signs the grant**: the canonical unsigned grant object,
   under the domain-separated message of capability-grant/v1, with an
   `active` authority key.
5. **`grant.json` is embedded** at the package root. The payload digest is
   unchanged by construction.
6. **The artifact is finalised**: the compressed package bytes, grant
   included.
7. **The Hub signs the artifact** — Profile 1, over those exact bytes — when
   it distributes the package.

## Key custody and ceremony

The capability authority key follows the release signing ceremony documented
for [runtime-release-bundle/v1.md](../runtime-release-bundle/v1.md), rather
than inventing a second custody model: generated non-exportably in a
protected environment, operated with short-lived federated credentials, no
long-lived secrets on any developer machine, and ceremony evidence retained
outside the repository. The key is purpose-bound: it signs capability grants
and nothing else, and no other key — author, Hub, release — may sign one.

## Rotation

Both ends rotate without invalidating the catalogue at once, using the
ordered key statuses the registry already defines:

- **Authority key rotation**: a new key enters the registry as `active`; the
  outgoing key moves to `verify_only`, where every grant it issued keeps
  verifying while no new grant can be signed with it; it moves to `revoked`
  only when its signatures must stop being accepted — which fails closed for
  every grant it ever issued, and is therefore a compromise response, not a
  rotation step.
- **Release-anchor rotation**: the registry ships inside the runtime
  release, so changing its contents is a release, verified and installed
  through the release bundle's own signature chain. There is no channel that
  updates the anchor outside a release.

The capability-grant corpus already exercises both sides of the ordered
statuses: `verify_only_key_admitted` is the rotated-anchor case — a grant
signed before rotation, verifying after it — and `revoked_key` is the
retired-key case, failing closed.

## Revocation without a network dependency

capability-grant/v1 is deliberate that a grant has no expiry and no online
revocation check. That must not be read as "a mistakenly issued or
compromised grant is valid forever." Withdrawal is by **denial list inside a
signed runtime release**:

```json
{
  "schema": "ori.capability_denial.v1",
  "entries": [
    { "kind": "grant", "sha256": "sha256:<64-lowercase-hex>" },
    { "kind": "payload", "sha256": "sha256:<64-lowercase-hex>" }
  ]
}
```

- The document is strict JSON: exactly these fields, unknown or duplicate
  fields refused, `kind` exactly `grant` or `payload`, `sha256` exactly
  `sha256:<64-lowercase-hex>`.
- **Carriage is fixed and mandatory.** The list ships at the release-owned
  path `capability-denial.json` inside the release bundle, listed in
  `BUNDLE-MANIFEST.json`, in **every** release — a release with no denials
  ships the explicit valid empty list, which is the only representation of
  "no denials". No configuration key, environment variable, package content,
  or fleet selector may supply, replace, or relocate it.
- **Absence and malformation fail closed at the grant-admission join.** A
  malformed list fails the release build that would ship it; if a defective
  or missing artifact nonetheless reaches a device — a signed release is not
  proof its CI ran — the consumer MUST NOT proceed as though nothing is
  denied. A missing, unreadable, or unparseable denial artifact makes every
  **grant-bearing** package unavailable (no-grant Tier A packages carry
  nothing a denial can name and are unaffected), and a hardened deployment
  fails startup on it. "Deny nothing" is never the meaning of a broken
  artifact, because that reading lets a defective release silently restore
  every denied capability.
- A `grant` entry denies the grant whose SHA-256 over its **canonical
  unsigned bytes** matches — the same bytes the authority signed, so the
  identity survives re-serialisation and cannot be dodged by whitespace.
- A `payload` entry denies **every** grant whose `payload_sha256` matches:
  the payload itself is condemned, whoever granted what over it.
- The list ships inside the signed release and carries no signature of its
  own: its authenticity is the release's.
- A consumer holding a denial list evaluates it after field validity and
  before key resolution in capability-grant/v1's order — the match needs
  only canonical bytes, no key material — and refuses a denied grant with a
  reason naming the denial, not the signature.

This is **slow by design**. The response time to a bad grant is a release
cycle, because the same channel that grants authority is the only channel
that withdraws it; anything faster would be an online dependency wearing a
different name. An operator who needs a bad package stopped today removes
the package; the denial list is what makes the removal permanent for a
fleet.

## Compromise response

If the authority key is compromised, three instruments compose, from blunt
to precise:

1. **Registry status.** Moving the key to `revoked` in the next release
   fails closed every grant it ever signed — legitimate ones included. This
   is the correct first move when the compromise window is unknown, because
   **previously issued grants are presumed suspect, not presumed valid**: a
   signature by a compromised key proves nothing about when it was made,
   and there is no trusted timestamp to partition before from after.
2. **Denial entries** restore precision afterwards, and re-issuance is a
   rebuild, not a re-signature: the replacement grant carries a new
   `authority_key_id` and signature, so `grant.json` changes, so the final
   package bytes change, so the Hub artifact signature over them must be
   regenerated. Recovery runs signing-order steps 4–7 for every legitimate
   grant — sign under the replacement key, embed, finalise the artifact, Hub
   re-signs — and republishes the rebuilt artifact; a consumer holds the
   replacement only after that. The payload tree digest excludes the grant
   and is unchanged, which is what lets the review rest on the existing
   audit records rather than re-examining the payload. Any grant the
   attacker issued stays dead with the key.
3. **Anchor rotation** ships the replacement `active` key in the same
   release.

## Vectors

`denial-vectors-v1.json` is the normative corpus for the denial document,
checked in CI by `scripts/check-capability-grant-issuance-vectors`, which
shares no code with whatever authored it and needs no key material — denial
is digest matching. Its denial targets are not synthetic: the denied digests
are computed from the capability-grant corpus's own admitted case, so the
vectors prove a denial list denies a grant that genuinely verifies. The
corpus covers at minimum: an empty list denying nothing; a grant denied by
grant digest; a grant denied by payload digest; a near-miss digest denying
nothing; refusal of a malformed list — wrong schema, unknown field,
duplicate key, bad entry kind, bad digest format; the grant-admission join
for a broken artifact — a missing and an unparseable denial artifact each
making grant-bearing packages unavailable while leaving a no-grant package
unaffected; and the audit-record fixtures — an accepted record, and
refusals for malformed records and for a granted ceiling exceeding its
request. The rotated-anchor and retired-key cases live in the
capability-grant corpus, named above; the no-grant admission branch is
vectored there too, where the archive manifests live.

## Downstream impact

- **ori-runtime** ships the denial list with its release, evaluates it at
  the stage named above, and implements the implicit Tier A ceiling for
  no-grant packages.
- **ori-skills-hub** carries grants it did not sign and may not sign;
  nothing here changes its distribution role.
- **ori-sdk-python** surfaces the requested-versus-granted ceiling to
  authors and refuses at build time a manifest that exceeds what issuance
  granted — or, with no grant, a manifest that declares above Tier A.
